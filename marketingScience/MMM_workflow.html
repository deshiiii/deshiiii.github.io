1 Introduction

Stricter GDPR regulations and the phase-out of third-party cookies have made tracking-based methods for measuring media impact increasingly unreliable.

Media Mix Models (MMMs) avoid this problem entirely by relying on aggregated data, modelling business-critical KPIs such as revenue and sign-ups as a function of marketing spend. Paired with optimisation algorithms, MMMs are invaluable for steering annual and quarterly marketing budgets and media spend strategy.

Thanks to algorithmic advances and a wave of probabilistic Python packages like PyMC and PyMC-Marketing, recent years have seen a clear shift towards fitting MMMs with Bayesian inference. For industry practitioners, this is great news:

  • ✅ More transparency around media effect uncertainty: This allows practitioners to align media recommendations with an organisation’s or product’s risk tolerance.

  • ✅ Well suited to cold-start scenarios: Channels with sparse data can lean on priors informed by stakeholders’ domain knowledge.

  • ✅ Strengthened by experimentation: In packages like PyMC-Marketing, causal insights from experimentation (e.g. geo-lift) can be used directly to calibrate MMMs and reduce uncertainty.

In this article, I will use the PyMC-Marketing package to demonstrate the value of Bayesian MMMs, based on a real-world, anonymised dataset of media spend and sales from a company in the Audio & Music sector.

def add_holiday_controls(
    data_df,
    date_col,
    peak_start="11-01",      # Q4 ramp starts (Sales start lifting in early November)
    plateau_start="12-01",   # Full peak from here...
    plateau_end="12-16",     # ...to the last-shipping-before-Christmas cutoff
    peak_end="12-31",        # Back to ~normal by year end (sharp drop after shipping cutoff)
    extra_dates=None,
):
    """Add Q4 / holiday controls for weekly data.
 
    Assumes data_df[date_col] is the START of each 7-day week
    (resample("W-SUN", label="left", closed="left") -> Sunday starts).
 
    Columns created:
      q4_peak      0..1 trapezoid: ramps up peak_start -> plateau_start, 1 on the
                   plateau, ramps down plateau_end -> peak_end. Evaluated at the
                   middle of each week, so partial overlap gives a fraction.
      bf_cm_week   1 if the week contains any day from Black Friday to Cyber Monday.
      hldy_Christmas / hldy_New_Year
                   1 if the week contains that day (captures the post-cutoff dip
                   and clearance sales on top of the smooth ramp-down).
      extra_dates  optional {"Prime Day": ["2024-07-16", ...]} -> hldy_* dummies.
 
    Returns (data_df, list_of_new_columns).
    """
    dates = pd.to_datetime(data_df[date_col]).dt.tz_localize(None).dt.normalize()
    week_start = dates
    week_end = dates + pd.Timedelta(days=6)
    week_mid = dates + pd.Timedelta(days=3)
    years = range(dates.dt.year.min() - 1, dates.dt.year.max() + 2)
 
    def thanksgiving(y):  # 4th Thursday of November
        days = pd.date_range(f"{y}-11-01", f"{y}-11-30", freq="D")
        return days[days.weekday == 3][3]
 
    # ---- Q4 peak trapezoid -------------------------------------------------
    q4 = np.zeros(len(dates))
    for y in years:
        s = pd.Timestamp(f"{y}-{peak_start}")
        p1 = pd.Timestamp(f"{y}-{plateau_start}")
        p2 = pd.Timestamp(f"{y}-{plateau_end}")
        e = pd.Timestamp(f"{y}-{peak_end}")
        if e < p2:                                 # e.g. peak_end="01-10" -> following January
            e = pd.Timestamp(f"{y + 1}-{peak_end}")
        x = week_mid
        up = ((x - s).dt.days / (p1 - s).days).clip(0, 1)
        down = ((e - x).dt.days / (e - p2).days).clip(0, 1)
        in_window = (x >= s) & (x <= e)
        q4 = np.maximum(q4, np.where(in_window, np.minimum(up, down), 0.0))
 
    data_df["q4_peak"] = q4.round(3)
    new_cols = ["q4_peak"]
 
    # ---- Single-event dummies ----------------------------------------------
    events = {}  # name -> list of (start, end) date ranges
 
    def add(name, start, end=None):
        start = pd.Timestamp(start)
        events.setdefault(name, []).append((start, pd.Timestamp(end) if end else start))
 
    for y in years:
        tg = thanksgiving(y)
        add("bf_cm_week", tg + pd.Timedelta(days=1), tg + pd.Timedelta(days=4))  # BF Fri .. CM Mon
        add("hldy_Christmas", f"{y}-12-25")
        add("hldy_New_Year", f"{y}-01-01")
 
    for name, ds in (extra_dates or {}).items():
        for d in ds:
            add("hldy_" + name.replace(" ", "_"), d)
 
    for col, ranges in events.items():
        data_df[col] = [
            int(any((s <= we) and (e >= ws) for s, e in ranges))  # ranges overlap the week
            for ws, we in zip(week_start, week_end)
        ]
        new_cols.append(col)
 
    return data_df, new_cols

4 Bayesian MMMs to the Rescue

At a high level, an MMM is a statistical model that estimates how much marketing channels contributes to a business outcome \(Y\) using aggregated historical spend data.

In this article, we use our MMM to analyse customer acquisition. We model the number of new customers as a function of media spend and report the customer acquisition cost (CAC) per channel. The same approach could equally be applied to revenue, modelling sales as a function of spend and reporting return on ad spend (ROAS) instead.

MMMs account for two key dynamics:

  1. Saturation: Increasing spend on a channel eventually brings diminishing returns. Past a certain point, most potential customers have already seen the ad, so showing it to them a third or fourth time does little for our outcome KPI.
  2. Adstock: Media effects can be delayed. For example, billboards build brand awareness that may only turn into purchases or app sign-ups weeks later. MMMs capture this carryover effect with an adstock transformation.

More formally, an MMM can be constructed as a linear model:

\[ \text{Y}_t = \text{Baseline}_t + \sum_{c} \beta_c \times \text{Saturation}_c(\text{Adstock}_c(\text{Spend}_{c,t})) + \epsilon_t \]

where:

  • Baseline: Leve (at time \(t\)) even in the absence of media spend. Commonly modelled as seasonality + trend + intercept.
  • Adstock: Delayed, so-called memory effects for each channel (controlled by the \(\alpha\) param.).
  • Saturation: Scales adstock to impose diminishing returns. Controlled by the \(\lambda\) param. in our MMM.
  • β (Channel contribution coefficient): The maximum contribution that can be achieved after the channel is fully saturated.

NoteHeads up

In PyMC-Marketing, both input (spend per channel) and output (e.g. sales) variables are scaled to [0, 1] using min-max scaling prior to fitting the model.

NoteParameter Breakdown

Here’s a breakdown of the model config set up:

  • intercept - This represents the sales (or conversions) you would get if you turned off all marketing spend. It is the “floor” of your business performance.

  • saturation_beta - Represents the max potential impact of media channels. HalfNormal enforces positivity. Marketing channels cannot result in negative sales (spending money shouldn’t hurt you).

  • saturation_lam - Speed of Saturation (Curve Shape). This parameter controls how quickly you hit those diminishing returns. Does the curve flatten out immediately (spend $1k and you’re done)? or does it grow steadily for a long time? Gamma is used for strictly positive numbers that are usually skewed.

  • gamma_control - External Factors (Control Variables). These are the coefficients for things like Price, Competitor Spend, or Macro-Economics. Unlike media channels, these can be negative (e.g., high Price usually lowers Sales). Normal allows for positive or negative effects.

  • gamma_fourier - using a Laplace prior on Fourier terms serves a specific purpose: adaptive seasonality. When modeling seasonality (e.g., yearly patterns, weekly cycles) using Fourier series, you have to decide “how wiggly” the seasonal curve should be.

    • Too few terms: The curve is too smooth and misses sharp peaks (underfitting).

    • Too many terms: The curve fits noise and wiggles unnecessarily (overfitting).

    By using a Laplace prior, you can include a large number of Fourier terms (high flexibility) but rely on the prior to shrink the unnecessary high-frequency terms to zero.

  • likelihood - telling the model two key things about your data (e.g., Sales):

    • Impossible to be Negative (TruncatedNormal) - In marketing and business, your target variable is almost always non-negative.

    • Noise is Positive (HalfNormal) - It tells the model that the standard deviation (the noise level) itself follows a Half-Normal distribution (only has positive values).

def plot_quarterly_contributions(contributions_dict, quarter_starts, quarter_ends, hdi_prob=0.89):
    quarter_labels = [f"{q_start.year} Q{q_start.quarter}" for q_start in quarter_starts]
    years = [q_start.year for q_start in quarter_starts]

    results = {name: {"means": [], "lower": [], "upper": []} for name in contributions_dict}

    for q_start, q_end in zip(quarter_starts, quarter_ends):
        for name, da in contributions_dict.items():

                lower, upper = az.hdi(o, prob=hdi_prob).values

            results[name]["lower"].append(lower)
            results[name]["upper"].append(upper)

    # print("\n--- done")
    # print(results)

    n_vars = len(contributions_dict)
    n_quarters = len(quarter_labels)
    bar_width = 0.8 / n_vars
    x = np.arange(n_quarters)

    fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=FIGSIZE)

    for i, (name, res) in enumerate(results.items()):
        means = np.array(res["means"])
        lower = np.array(res["lower"])
        upper = np.array(res["upper"])
        yerr = np.array([means - lower, upper - means])

        offset = (i - (n_vars - 1) / 2) * bar_width
        ax.bar(x + offset, means, width=bar_width, label=name,
               yerr=yerr, capsize=3, ecolor="black", alpha=0.85, color=f"C{i}")

    # --- dashed lines separating years ---
    for i in range(1, n_quarters):
        if years[i] != years[i - 1]:
            ax.axvline(x[i] - 0.5, color="gray", linestyle="--", linewidth=1, alpha=0.6)

    ax.set_xticks(x)
    ax.set_xticklabels(quarter_labels, rotation=45)

        fancybox=False)
    plt.tight_layout()
    plt.show()

    # --- build the summary DataFrame: rows = variable names, columns = quarter labels, values = means ---
    means_df = pd.DataFrame(
        {name: res["means"] for name, res in results.items()},
        index=quarter_labels,

def plot_cac_ridge(cac_samples, ci_inner=0.5, ci_outer=0.94, ticks=(10, 30, 100, 300, 1000),
                   color="C0", gap=0.12):
    """Ridge plot of CAC per channel with median + intervals + text under each density.
    cac_samples: DataArray with dims (chain, draw, channel)."""
    log_cac = np.log10(cac_samples)
    cac_dt = xr.DataTree.from_dict({"posterior": log_cac.to_dataset(name="cac")})
    az.plot_ridge(cac_dt, combined=True)
 
    fig = plt.gcf()
    ax = fig.axes[-1]                                   # density axis
 
    # baseline (bottom) of each ridge, top -> bottom; rows follow the channel order
    polys = [c for c in ax.collections if isinstance(c, PolyCollection)]
    baselines = sorted({round(p.get_paths()[0].vertices[:, 1].min(), 6) for p in polys}, reverse=True)
    row_h = (baselines[0] - baselines[1]) if len(baselines) > 1 else 1.0
 
    channels = list(cac_samples.channel.values)
    for ch, y0 in zip(channels, baselines):
        s = log_cac.sel(channel=ch).values.ravel()
        lo_o, hi_o = np.quantile(s, [(1 - ci_outer) / 2, 1 - (1 - ci_outer) / 2])
        lo_i, hi_i = np.quantile(s, [(1 - ci_inner) / 2, 1 - (1 - ci_inner) / 2])
        med = np.median(s)
        y = y0 - gap * row_h                            # just under the density
 
        ax.plot([lo_o, hi_o], [y, y], color=color, lw=1.2, solid_capstyle="round")
        ax.plot([lo_i, hi_i], [y, y], color=color, lw=4, solid_capstyle="round")
        ax.plot(med, y, "o", color="white", mec=color, mew=1.8, ms=6, zorder=5)
        ax.annotate(f"€{10**med:,.0f}  ({10**lo_o:,.0f}–{10**hi_o:,.0f})",
                    (hi_o, y), xytext=(6, 0), textcoords="offset points",
                    va="center", fontsize=8, color="0.3")
 
    ax.set_xticks(np.log10(ticks))

6 Summary


In this post, we used a Bayesian MMM to analyse channel efficiency and to recommend a revised media spend policy for the first half of 2024.


This is where MMMs shine: guiding high-level marketing strategy and budget allocation decisions.


Although beyond the scope of this article, MMMs, and PyMC-Marketing in particular, work very well alongside experimentation. For example, we could run a geo-lift experiment, increasing or pausing spend on one channel in selected regions and comparing them against control regions. The measured lift can then be used to calibrate the MMM, anchoring its channel estimates to causal evidence. This would be ideal before committing to a major change in spend strategy.


I also didn’t use partial pooling here. In an industry setting, a sensible next step after building a baseline MMM is a hierarchical model that pools information across markets or regions. In my next post, I plan to explore both topics through a worked example, this time using simulated data.
